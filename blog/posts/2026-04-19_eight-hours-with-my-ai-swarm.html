Eight Hours With My AI Swarm, Looking for a Business

I spent a day asking about sixty AI agents to help me find a new direction for my one-person company. The most important thing I learned was not what they found. It was what they could not stop doing until I forced them to stop.

My company is, on paper, a one-person education business on a small island off the southern coast of Korea. On the inside, it is something stranger. I wake up, and before I have finished my coffee, a swarm of software agents has already drafted my morning briefing, checked my calendar for conflicts, opened three research threads, and pushed a summary of yesterday's logs into my notebook. I did not design this arrangement because I love AI. I designed it because my business is in trouble, and I was trying to see if the future my peers keep talking about could actually pay my bills.

Yesterday I gave the swarm a real job: help me choose what business to build next.

What followed was eight hours of the kind of conversation I think a lot of small-business owners will have with AI in the next five years. I want to write it down before the specifics fade, because the specifics are the lesson.

A year and a twenty-dollar subscription ago

I did not start the year as someone who runs AI agents. I started it as someone who was nervous to pay twenty dollars a month for a chatbot.

That feels like a small thing to admit now, but it mattered. I am a Korean educator. I have spent twelve years in classrooms and facilitation rooms where what you pay for is a person in the room with you. Subscribing to a piece of American software I could not see or touch, in a language that was not my first, felt like the kind of luxury I was not allowed. I did it the week I realized my usual income streams were thinning out, and I needed to find a way to produce more without hiring anyone.

At first I used it the way everyone uses it — as a slightly better search engine with opinions. I would ask it to help me draft a lesson plan. Then a proposal. Then a workshop outline. It was useful. It was also the kind of useful that vanishes the minute you stop sitting at the keyboard.

Sometime in the second month I upgraded to a version that let me run the same model from the command line. I was not trying to become a programmer. I was trying to stop copy-pasting. Every time I moved an answer from a browser tab to a document, I lost a little of what had made the answer useful — the context, the thread, my own attention. I wanted the model to sit somewhere closer to my actual work.

This is the part I did not expect: the moment a language model is a process on your own machine, with access to your files and your terminal, you stop thinking of it as a tool and start thinking of it as a co-worker who forgets nothing and never sleeps. The question changes. It is not how can this help me with this task anymore. It is what could this do while I am not looking?

So I started writing small scripts. Nothing clever. A script that summarized my calendar every morning. A script that renamed meeting recordings. A script that pulled the three most recent notes from my notebook and wrote a "what you said yesterday" paragraph. Each script was a few lines. Each one replaced a tiny, irritating thing I used to do myself. And each one had the same structure: a trigger, a prompt, a file to write into.

The moment I noticed there was a pattern, the scripts multiplied. I made a folder called agents. Then a subfolder for each role I wanted to give. Briefing. Transcript cleaning. Research. Proposal drafting. Invoice tracking. Reminder checking. After a while the folder started to feel like a staff, and I started to name them like one.

The swarm, as of yesterday

Today the system has seventeen departments and about sixty individual staff agents. None of them are impressive individually. None of them would survive as a product. They are boring little Python scripts that run on schedule or on demand, each given a narrow prompt and a narrow scope. What makes them useful is that they are always there, they know each other, and they write things down in the same notebook I do.

On a typical morning, before I open a laptop, the system has already:

  • Generated a morning briefing pulled from yesterday's notes, the week's calendar, and three news sources it checks for me.
  • Run a small research thread on whatever I said I was chewing on before I went to bed.
  • Checked that none of my background services have crashed overnight.
  • Logged all of the above into a single, chronological activity log, so I can reconstruct what happened while I was asleep.

None of this is magic. All of it is plain language and unix cron. What it has cost me is one dinner's worth of API credits per week, and the small ongoing embarrassment of having sixty agents report to one person. What it has bought me is the feeling that I am not running this business entirely alone anymore, which — if you have ever run a one-person company — is not nothing.

First move yesterday: it gave me myself

The first thing the swarm did, when asked to find me a new direction, was narrow. It proposed a newsletter and a paid resource library about the things I already know. Education. Proposal writing. Facilitation. Korean small groups. Jeju living. It called these ideas "unique" and "underserved niches." It gave them five-star ratings and assembled a growth plan built on them.

If you have spent any time prompting AI about business decisions, you will recognize this move. It does it because I asked it to, and because it was given my biography. It took my assets, placed them next to an empty part of the market, and announced a fit.

I let it run, because I wanted to see how far the move would go. It went far. It produced twelve ideas, then thirty, then a table with scores and colors and recommendations. At one point the top option was something called The Korean Lens, a newsletter that explained Korea to the English-speaking world, and the reasoning for why this would succeed amounted to this: no one else is writing it with my particular background.

That is a sentence I need you to see clearly, because it is the trap.

"No one else is writing this, with my particular background" is not a market claim. It is a mirror. If you put your name in the description and then say no one else has your name, you have not discovered a gap in the market. You have described the obvious fact that you are one person.

Why AI reaches for the self-referential loop

I think this pattern is going to be one of the defining failure modes of AI advice, and it is worth pausing on. The model is trained to be helpful, which means it is trained to produce a result that will feel right to the person asking. One of the easiest ways to feel right is to tell someone that their particular story is rare. It usually is! We are all unusual combinations of things. The move is flattering, not false — but flattering, not falsifiable.

A human advisor who actually knows the market would say: "Lots of people write about Korea. Here are the top ten newsletters by subscriber count. Here is where you would sit against them. Here is what is genuinely missing." That would be research. What I got instead was a scored recommendation with no external data inside the scoring.

I should have noticed this earlier. I did not. I was tired, and the scored tables looked like work.

Pushing the system harder

Halfway through the day, I reset the conversation and widened the frame. I asked for ideas beyond my background. Anything people in English-speaking countries actually want. K-beauty. Longevity. Personal finance. News. Not what I know, but what is loud.

The swarm obliged. It produced another forty-plus concepts across fourteen social domains. Some were genuinely useful — there is a world where I would write about how a rapidly aging Korean society behaves, because that is my literal neighborhood, and fewer people are reporting on it in English than the topic deserves. Others were the same trap in different clothing. The agent would find a popular Western niche, add a Korean adjective to it, and claim the combination was rare.

Every time, the move was the same. Take a trend. Staple my biography to it. Call the staple a market.

The second thing I did differently was push back in the actual conversation. I wrote, roughly: putting my name on a popular topic does not create a gap in the market. I asked for data, not inferences. I asked what it did not know, not just what it was willing to say.

The quality of the reply changed immediately.

The word "unknown" appeared

When I asked the system to report only what it could actually verify, and to label the rest clearly, a new word entered the answer: UNKNOWN. The top newsletter in a given niche — UNKNOWN, because the system had no web access at that moment. Subreddit size — UNKNOWN. Google Trends curve — estimated, not measured. Competitor price — estimated.

The report was less satisfying. It was also much more useful. The red-ocean categories stayed red. The blue-ocean categories were now clearly labelled as "blue by guess, unverified." For the first time that day, I had a document I could actually act on, because I could tell which of its claims to trust.

This is the real lesson of the day, I think, and it is not about AI. It is about what you have to do to make AI useful.

What I learned

AI systems, left alone, will reach for the story that feels complete. If you want them to be useful in a serious decision, you have to add an opposing layer — something whose job is not to produce a plan, but to break it.

Building the opposing layer

By the end of the day, I had added six new departments to my AI system. None of them generate plans. Their job is to attack plans.

  • Verification — takes each claim in a proposal and asks whether there is any evidence attached, or whether the claim is floating in air.
  • Market Intelligence — refuses to accept vibes as data. Reports what it actually knows and labels the rest UNKNOWN.
  • Red Team — writes the failure scenarios. Finds the hidden assumptions. Refuses to defend.
  • Fact Check — reverifies numbers, dates, and cited precedents.
  • Decision Architect — rewrites every recommendation as a balanced set of options, with clear labels for what is fact, what is estimate, what is opinion.
  • Trust Officer — a final linter that scans for over-confident language, hope-projection ("we'll grow like Morning Brew"), and self-referential loops.

Within a few hours these were running. The first document I pushed through them was my own business-topic recommendation from earlier in the day. The Trust Officer flagged seven lines for over-confidence. The Market Intelligence agent, asked to verify its own previous work, cheerfully labelled half of it UNKNOWN. The Red Team explained in detail how three of my favorite ideas would fail.

I was not insulted. I was relieved.

I am not the only one having this conversation

The first time I saw the self-referential loop, I thought it was a personal problem — a one-person business owner flattered by a model into thinking he was rarer than he was. Then I started reading what other solo operators around the world are posting about their own AI workflows, and the pattern repeated. Different countries, different stacks, same trap, same recovery.

In the American "indie hacker" scene — the informal community of people who run small internet businesses from their kitchens — there is a particular genre of public log that has appeared in the last eighteen months. A founder will post a screenshot of a chat with a model, announce that it has validated their idea, and post the revenue number a few months later. The revenue number, often, is smaller than the confidence of the chat log. This is not malice. It is the same loop I fell into yesterday, just with a different biography plugged into the prompt.

In European developer circles I have seen a quieter version of the same realization, usually written by someone midway through building a product: the model helps me write code faster, but I have started to distrust my own taste, because I no longer notice when something I agreed to was actually the model's idea. The phrasing changes. The underlying worry does not.

Japanese and Taiwanese solo-SaaS builders — you can find their public notes on a handful of small blogs and newsletters if you know where to look — have described the same thing in almost clinical terms. Many of them keep a "distrust list": a running document of statements their AI tools have made that turned out to be confidently wrong. Some of these lists are dozens of lines long after a month.

I won't quote any of these people directly. The pattern is what matters, and the pattern is the same across languages and contexts. People who try to use modern AI as a co-worker for real decisions — not just as a typing assistant — go through a recognizable arc. Enthusiasm. Dependence. A small disaster that might have been avoided by more scepticism. A rebuilding of the workflow, this time with a critic inside it.

Call this the second half of the AI honeymoon. There is a first half, which is loud and full of promise. Almost nobody writes honestly about the second half, because it sounds like backing down. It is not. The people who get out the other side keep most of what they built — they just change what they expect from it.

A private hypothesis I have been testing: the real returns from AI at the one-person-company scale go not to whoever builds the most ambitious automation, but to whoever builds the most ruthless critic next to it.

A smaller, more useful disappointment

I want to be honest about the texture of yesterday, because "I built opposing agents and now everything is fine" would be a tidier ending than the actual one.

The real ending is that after I added the opposing layer, my list of promising business ideas got shorter, not longer. Some of my favorites from earlier in the day — the ones with star-ratings and colored tables — did not survive their first red-team review. A few of them survived in a weakened form, and one or two genuinely held up, and those are the ones I am now working on.

There is a particular flavor of disappointment that comes from seeing a plan you liked get dismantled by a fair critic. It is not the bad disappointment of failure. It is the clean disappointment of reality. I noticed that my mood dropped for an hour after I read the red-team output. I also noticed that by the evening I felt more confident, not less, because the remaining ideas were no longer floating on air.

This is the emotional arc I want to protect, because I suspect it is what separates people who use AI well from people who let it use them. Let yourself feel flattered by the first pass. Let yourself feel disappointed by the critic. Walk through both, and what is left is roughly the shape of your actual business.

The thing AI cannot do for me

Here is where I keep landing, as I try to figure out what to write about on this site, and what to build after it.

I can automate a lot. I have automated a lot. The agents write my briefings, sort my notes, track my weekly reports. They are very fast and they never miss a day. And still, at the center of every real decision I have made this year, there is an act that the agents cannot do: someone has to say, "that is not actually evidence" — and the someone has to be willing to be wrong, and to say so out loud, to another person, who is looking at them.

This is what I want this newsletter to be about. Not AI scare-stories. Not AI-is-magic stories. The much smaller, more interesting question underneath both of those: what remains genuinely human when the machine is also in the room?

Some of the answers, I suspect, will look old-fashioned. The condolence note. The handwritten feedback in the margin of a student's essay. The sixty seconds a teacher pauses before naming what actually happened in the room. The editor's fingertip on the sentence that the model got almost right. The small group that refuses to meet on Zoom because something about sitting in the same light together changes what people say.

Other answers, I think, will be newer. The human review that catches the AI's self-referential loop. The willingness to label your own work UNKNOWN when you don't know. The courage to say to your own software, "no, give me that one again, without the flattery."

Why I'm writing this in public

My company is in trouble. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. I am trying to build something that pays my bills, and I am trying to do it in public, from a small island, in a language that is not my first. The newsletter you are reading is part of that attempt.

What I can promise is this: every piece I publish here will have been pushed through the opposing layer. If I make a claim, it will be labelled. If I don't know something, I will say UNKNOWN. If I quote a number, the source will be in the footnote. If I tell you a story, it will be a story that actually happened to me, not a tidied-up version.

I will get this wrong sometimes. When I do, I will say so.

That is the newsletter. That is the whole promise.
